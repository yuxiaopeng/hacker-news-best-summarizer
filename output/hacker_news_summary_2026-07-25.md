## 8. 抹香鲸吹泡泡以达到安稳的垂直睡眠。

**原文标题**: Sperm Whales blow bubbles to achieve restful, vertical sleep

**原文链接**: [https://news.st-andrews.ac.uk/archive/sperm-whales-blow-bubbles-to-achieve-restful-vertical-sleep/](https://news.st-andrews.ac.uk/archive/sperm-whales-blow-bubbles-to-achieve-restful-vertical-sleep/)

New research from the Universities of St Andrews and Neuchâtel reveals how sperm whales achieve restful, vertical sleep just beneath the ocean surface: by blowing bubbles. Sperm whales are unique in their vertical resting posture, which protects them from surface waves without expending energy to dive deep.

Published in the *Journal of Experimental Biology*, the study explains that sperm whales are naturally positively buoyant due to spermaceti oil. To counteract this natural buoyancy and the expansion of lung gas while resting, they release gas bubbles. This precise regulation helps them achieve neutral buoyancy and remain submerged.

Researchers used small suction-cup tags on sperm whales off the Norwegian coast to record sound and 3D movements. The tags detected distinct bubble sounds, and a simulation confirmed that bubble release effectively reduced positive buoyancy, allowing the whales to stay submerged while resting. The findings also suggest that whales start resting dives with less gas volume compared to deep foraging dives.

Professor Patrick Miller highlighted the fascinating ability of sperm whales to exquisitely control their buoyancy, making fine-scale adjustments even while asleep. The authors also hypothesize that this bubble-releasing behavior might be linked to the off-gassing of excess CO2 or N2 from tissues, potentially having implications for metabolic gas exchange.

## 12. I Tried Building a Real App with AI. It Took a Year

**原文标题**: I Tried Building a Real App with AI. It Took a Year

**原文链接**: [https://www.alexhyett.com/videos/tried-building-app-with-ai-it-took-a-year/](https://www.alexhyett.com/videos/tried-building-app-with-ai-it-took-a-year/)

Alex Hyett chronicles his year-long endeavor to build a habit tracking app, HabitTed, using AI, driven by the inability to find an existing app that met his specific requirements (e.g., long lists, no cloud storage, gen-emojis, flexible goals, notes) and a reluctance to pay high subscription fees for alternatives like Grit.

Though new to Swift, Hyett used AI (Cursor) and initially developed a functional app within just 6 hours, complete with core features and iCloud syncing. However, he soon discovered the AI-generated code was deeply flawed: messy, inefficient (e.g., 1000-line views, inconsistent styling), and full of bugs like a non-functional iCloud sync and performance issues. He felt he learned little during this initial phase.

Consequently, Hyett spent many months manually refactoring the entire codebase, fixing critical bugs, optimizing performance, and adding missing features. He also encountered problems with the release of iOS 26, which AI couldn't help with due to its newness, forcing him to solve issues independently.

After a year of development and extensive testing, he successfully launched HabitTed, now a maintainable app. While AI provided a kickstart, Hyett concludes that it "gets you 80% there, but the last 20% will take you 80% of the time," especially if you don't understand the underlying code. He warns against over-reliance on AI, stating it hinders skill acquisition and raises concerns about the future of software development, potentially leading to a workforce losing core coding abilities or suffering burnout from constantly cleaning "AI slop."
